Jet Card vs. Charter: Which Fits How You Fly?

By Matt Caruso · Published May 10, 2026 · Updated October 4, 2026

A jet card can be a good fit. So can charter. I’ve sold both, and I’d start with your trips before picking a product. Here’s what to compare.

Start with the trips, then the product

A jet card can make repeat booking easier. Charter lets you arrange one trip at a time. Neither wins automatically because you fly a certain number of hours.

The useful question is: what would each option look like for your actual travel? Your usual route, a holiday trip, and the flight that needs a different cabin. Compare those before you commit.

What you are buying

On-demand charter: you request a trip, review an aircraft and quote, and book under that trip’s terms. You do not need to buy a block of hours first. A deposit or advance payment may still be required for the booking.

Jet card: you prepay for flight hours or funds and use them under a program’s pricing and service rules. The agreement defines what access you have, how bookings work, and which costs are included.

When I would compare each option

If your trips are occasional or hard to predict, start by looking at charter. You can learn what cabin, luggage space, and booking process suit you without making a program commitment first.

If you fly regularly and want established booking terms, put a jet card alongside those charter quotes. Look at what the program actually promises for the routes and dates you use.

A mix can make sense too. You might use a card for recurring travel and charter for a trip that needs a different aircraft or falls outside the program’s service area. That is a comparison to make, not an automatic recommendation.

The questions I would ask before buying a card

  • What is included? Ask which taxes, fees, and additional charges apply to your sample trips.
  • How much notice is required? Compare ordinary dates with peak dates, and ask what happens if you request a flight inside that window.
  • What are the daily minimums? A short flight can be billed differently from its actual time in the air.
  • Where can I fly? Check the service area and terms for trips outside it.
  • Can I change aircraft categories? Ask how a larger or smaller cabin affects your balance and pricing.
  • What happens to unused hours or funds? Expiration and refund rules vary. Get the answer from the agreement.
  • What happens if plans change? Read cancellation, substitution, and aircraft recovery terms before you need them.

A fixed hourly rate is one part of the decision. The booking rules matter just as much if they determine whether the program fits your schedule.

Compare a real set of trips

  1. List your recent routes, dates, passengers, and luggage.
  2. Pick several representative trips, including a peak-date trip if you take one.
  3. Request charter quotes and ask how the card would price and handle those same trips.
  4. Compare the total trip cost, cabin, notice requirements, cancellation terms, and support.
  5. Decide whether the convenience and program commitments are worth the upfront payment for you.

Who actually operates the flight?

The person arranging your flight and the company operating it can be different. Ask for the operating carrier and verify its charter authority. A card or brokerage brand does not replace that step. The FAA provides information on certificated Part 135 operators.
